How we calculate this

We estimate how much electricity your premises use in every half-hour of a typical year, based on the building type, floor area and opening hours (or your own bills, if you add them). For offices, the daily electricity pattern comes from measured meter data: recent half-hourly data from a London office (2023–25), blended with 36 European office buildings from the Building Data Genome Project 2. The heating pattern comes from the same building's half-hourly boiler gas data, so heat pump electricity falls when heating is actually needed. Other building types use a simpler pattern based on opening hours until measured data is added. Each upgrade changes that pattern: LED lighting lowers use while you're open, solar panels generate during daylight, a battery moves use from one time of day to another, and a heat pump replaces boiler fuel with electricity.

Each half-hour of change is multiplied by the carbon intensity of the electricity grid in your region at that time of day, in that month, using 2025 data. This matters because grid power is usually cleaner in the middle of the day and dirtier in winter evenings, so the same kilowatt-hour saved can be worth very different amounts of carbon. Solar power you send to the grid is counted as displacing grid power at the time it's exported.

Energy attribute certificates (EACs) record changes in consumption, not only renewable generation, so they capture what happens behind the meter that grid-level certificates like REGOs miss. We count electricity saved by LED lighting, solar power used on site and electricity a battery moves to cleaner times. Exported solar is left out to avoid double counting with REGOs. Certificates for heat pumps are still being developed; in the meantime, the gas or oil a heat pump avoids is shown as a separate option that could be sold for a fixed period.

Gas and oil use carbon factors from the UK government's greenhouse gas conversion factors. Building energy use starts from typical benchmarks for each type of premises, which vary a lot between real buildings, so these are indicative estimates only. An installer's quote and your actual meter data will give firmer figures.
